# Alternatives

## Current Decision-Making Process Without Our Tool

The specific decision we need to make is:

> Which occupations in California should receive priority funding for AI-related reskilling and workforce training programs?

This decision is important because California cannot provide the same amount of funding to every occupation. Some occupations may be more affected by AI, while others may have stronger employment growth, higher wages, more job openings, or a greater need for worker support. Therefore, I need to compare different occupations and decide where public funding could create the greatest benefit.

---

## What Could Trigger the Decision?

Several events could trigger this decision-making process.

First, the state or federal government might provide a new workforce-development grant. If new funding becomes available, EDD may need to decide which industries, occupations, regions, or training programs should receive the money.

Second, labor-market data might show that employment is declining in certain occupations. For example, automation or generative AI may reduce the need for some routine administrative tasks. If a large number of workers could be affected, EDD may need to consider whether those workers require reskilling.

Third, employers may report that job responsibilities are changing because of AI. A job may not completely disappear, but workers may need new technical, analytical, or communication skills to continue performing it successfully.

Fourth, local workforce development boards or community organizations may report that certain groups of workers are facing employment difficulties. These groups could include low-income workers, workers without a college degree, workers in rural areas, or workers from communities that have historically received fewer economic opportunities.

Finally, state leaders may establish new economic-development priorities. For example, California may want to expand employment in healthcare, clean energy, technology, advanced manufacturing, or other important industries. EDD may then need to support training programs connected to those priorities.

---

## Goals of the Decision

As the decision-maker, I would have several goals.

My first goal would be to help workers adapt to changes caused by AI instead of waiting until large numbers of workers lose their jobs.

My second goal would be to invest in occupations that have realistic employment opportunities. Funding training for an occupation would not be useful if employers are not hiring workers in that field.

My third goal would be to support programs that can realistically be delivered. A training program should have qualified instructors, appropriate learning materials, a reasonable completion time, and a clear connection to actual job opportunities.

My fourth goal would be to use public funding fairly. The decision should consider whether workers from different regions and demographic groups can access the training.

My final goal would be to produce a recommendation that can be explained to government leaders, employers, training providers, and the public.

---

## Information I Would Need

Without the proposed AI Workforce Investment Planner, I would need to collect information from several separate sources.

### 1. Employment Information

I would review the current number of workers in each occupation. An occupation with a large workforce may deserve more attention because changes to that occupation could affect many people.

I would also examine historical employment trends to determine whether employment in the occupation has been increasing, decreasing, or remaining stable.

### 2. Projected Job Demand

I would review occupational employment projections and the expected number of future job openings. This information would help me identify occupations that may provide realistic opportunities after workers complete training.

Job openings can come from both employment growth and replacement needs. Even if an occupation is not growing quickly, it may still have many openings because current workers retire or leave the occupation.

### 3. Wage Information

I would compare the median or average wages for different occupations. Higher wages may indicate that an occupation provides a stronger path to economic stability.

However, wage level would not be the only consideration. Some essential occupations may deserve support even if their wages are not among the highest.

### 4. AI Exposure and Task Changes

I would examine research about how AI could affect the tasks performed in each occupation.

I would try to determine whether AI is likely to:

* Replace some tasks performed by workers;
* Help workers complete tasks more efficiently;
* Create new responsibilities;
* Require workers to learn new software or technical skills;
* Change the number of workers employers need; or
* Create new occupations or job opportunities.

An occupation with high AI exposure would not automatically be classified as a declining occupation. In some cases, AI may support workers instead of replacing them. Therefore, I would need to distinguish between AI replacement and AI augmentation.

### 5. Education and Training Requirements

I would identify the education, licenses, certifications, and skills required for each occupation.

I would also estimate:

* How long the training would take;
* How much the training would cost;
* Whether workers could participate while employed;
* Whether online training would be possible;
* Whether qualified training providers already exist; and
* Whether workers could realistically move into the occupation after completing the program.

### 6. Regional Information

California has major regional differences. The labor market in Los Angeles may be different from the labor market in the Bay Area, Central Valley, Inland Empire, or rural Northern California.

I would examine whether the occupation has strong demand in specific regions and whether workers in those regions have access to training programs.

### 7. Equity Information

I would consider which groups of workers are currently employed in the occupation and which groups would benefit from the training investment.

This analysis could include:

* Income level;
* Educational background;
* Race and ethnicity;
* Gender;
* Age;
* Disability status;
* Veteran status;
* English-language ability; and
* Geographic location.

The goal would be to ensure that the funding does not only benefit workers who already have easy access to education and technology.

### 8. Previous Program Results

If similar workforce programs already exist, I would review their outcomes.

Important measures could include:

* Program enrollment;
* Program completion rate;
* Certification rate;
* Job-placement rate;
* Employment retention;
* Wage changes after training;
* Participant satisfaction; and
* Employer satisfaction.

Programs with strong results might be expanded, while programs with weak results might require changes before receiving additional funding.

## People and Organizations I Would Consult

Data alone would not provide enough information. I would also consult people who understand the occupations, workers, and training programs.

### Labor-Market Analysts

Labor-market analysts could help me interpret employment projections, wage data, job-opening estimates, and regional trends. They could also explain the limitations of the data.

### Employers

Employers could explain how AI is actually changing work inside their organizations. They could identify new skills they expect employees to have and describe whether they would hire workers who complete a proposed training program.

### Workers

Current workers could explain which tasks are changing, which skills they need, and what barriers might prevent them from participating in training.

### Labor Unions and Worker Organizations

These organizations could provide information about job quality, worker protection, workplace changes, and the concerns of employees affected by automation.

### Training Providers and Community Colleges

Training providers could explain whether a proposed program is realistic, how much it would cost, how long it would take, and what resources would be required.

### Local Workforce Development Boards

Local boards could provide regional knowledge about employer demand, unemployed workers, existing programs, and community needs.

### Community Organizations

Community organizations could help identify barriers involving transportation, childcare, language, internet access, or program awareness.

### Other Government Agencies

EDD may also need to coordinate with other state and local agencies responsible for education, economic development, labor policy, and social services.

## Possible Decision-Making Process Without Our Tool

Without the AI Workforce Investment Planner, I would probably complete the decision through the following steps.

### Step 1: Define the Purpose and Constraints

First, I would identify the purpose of the funding and any restrictions connected to it.

For example, I would need to know:

* The total amount of funding available;
* The deadline for spending the funding;
* Which workers are eligible;
* Which training costs can be covered;
* Whether the funding must support particular industries or regions; and
* What outcomes must be reported.

### Step 2: Create a List of Possible Occupations

Next, I would create an initial list of occupations that may deserve support.

The list could include occupations with:

* High AI exposure;
* Large numbers of workers;
* Strong projected demand;
* Many annual job openings;
* Good wages;
* Significant skill changes;
* A high risk of worker displacement; or
* Strong importance to California’s economy.

### Step 3: Collect Data from Different Sources

I would download employment, wage, projection, education, and demographic data from different websites or databases.

I might also collect research reports about AI exposure and employer surveys about changing skill requirements.

Because these sources may not be directly connected, I would probably store the information in several spreadsheets.

### Step 4: Clean and Match the Data

The data would need to be cleaned before comparison.

Different sources may use:

* Different occupation titles;
* Different versions of SOC occupation codes;
* Different time periods;
* Different geographic levels;
* Different definitions of AI exposure; and
* Different measurement methods.

I would need to match the occupation codes, remove duplicate information, check for missing values, and decide which year of data to use.

### Step 5: Compare Occupations

After preparing the data, I would compare the occupations using several criteria.

A simple comparison table might include:

| Criterion            | Question                                                   |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI exposure          | How strongly could AI change the occupation’s tasks?       |
| Workforce size       | How many California workers could be affected?             |
| Job demand           | Are employers expected to need workers in this occupation? |
| Job openings         | How many opportunities may be available each year?         |
| Wages                | Can the occupation provide economic stability?             |
| Training feasibility | Can useful training be delivered at a reasonable cost?     |
| Regional need        | Which California regions need support?                     |
| Equity impact        | Which workers and communities would benefit?               |
| Program evidence     | Have similar programs produced good outcomes?              |

### Step 6: Develop an Initial Ranking

I might assign a score to each occupation for every criterion.

For example, I could rate each occupation from 1 to 5 for AI exposure, workforce size, job demand, wages, training feasibility, and equity impact.

I would then calculate a total score. However, I would need to decide how much weight to give each factor. This could be difficult because different stakeholders may value different outcomes.

For example:

* Employers may prioritize occupations with immediate hiring needs.
* Worker organizations may prioritize occupations with high displacement risk.
* Government leaders may prioritize economic growth.
* Community groups may prioritize accessibility and equity.
* Training providers may prioritize programs that can be implemented quickly.

### Step 7: Consult Stakeholders

I would present the initial ranking to employers, workers, analysts, local workforce boards, and training providers.

I would ask whether the results match their experience and whether any important information is missing.

For example, the data may show strong demand for an occupation, but local employers may explain that the jobs require experience that a short training program cannot provide. In another case, an occupation may have moderate projected growth, but employers may report an urgent shortage of qualified workers.

### Step 8: Revise the Ranking

Based on stakeholder feedback, I would revise the scores, remove occupations that are not realistic training targets, and add occupations that were missed by the original data analysis.

I would document why each change was made so that the final decision would be easier to explain.

### Step 9: Recommend Funding Priorities

I would prepare a final recommendation that divides occupations into priority levels.

For example:

* **High priority:** Strong workforce need, clear AI-related skill changes, good employment opportunities, and feasible training;
* **Medium priority:** Some evidence of need, but additional research or a pilot program is required; and
* **Low priority:** Weak job demand, limited training feasibility, or insufficient evidence of worker benefit.

I might also recommend beginning with a small pilot program instead of immediately investing the full amount of funding.

### Step 10: Monitor the Results

After funding is distributed, I would track enrollment, completion, job placement, wage growth, and employer satisfaction.

The results would be used to decide whether the program should be continued, expanded, changed, or discontinued.

## Example of How I Might Make the Decision

Suppose I am comparing three occupations:

1. Administrative assistants;
2. Data analysts; and
3. Medical assistants.

Administrative assistants may have high AI exposure because generative AI can perform some writing, scheduling, and document-processing tasks. A large number of workers may be affected, so reskilling could be important. However, employment demand for the original occupation may not be strong enough to justify training workers only for the same job.

Data analysts may have growing demand and relatively high wages. AI may change how analysts perform their work, but workers who learn to use AI tools may become more productive. However, the training may require stronger technical preparation, which could make the program less accessible to some workers.

Medical assistants may have lower direct AI exposure, but healthcare employers may have strong demand for workers. AI training could still help medical assistants use new scheduling, documentation, or patient-management systems. The occupation may also provide an accessible pathway for workers who do not have a four-year degree.

I would not select an occupation based on only one measure. I would compare the number of workers affected, future job opportunities, wage levels, training requirements, regional demand, and equity impact before making a recommendation.

## Current Tools and Workarounds

Without our proposed tool, I would probably rely on:

* Government labor-market websites;
* Downloaded CSV or Excel files;
* Spreadsheets;
* Manually created comparison tables;
* Written reports about AI and employment;
* Email communication with stakeholders;
* Meetings and interviews;
* PowerPoint presentations; and
* Staff judgment.

These methods can still produce a decision, but they require staff to move between many sources and manually combine the information.

## Problems With the Current Alternative

### Data Is Scattered Across Different Sources

Employment, wages, projections, AI exposure, training requirements, and demographic information may all come from different sources. Staff may spend a large amount of time locating and combining the data.

### Occupation Codes May Not Match

One source may use a broad occupation category, while another uses a detailed SOC code. Some datasets may use an older version of the occupational classification system. Incorrect matching could produce misleading results.

### AI Information May Not Be California-Specific

Many studies about AI exposure use national data. National estimates may not accurately represent California’s industries, regional economy, or employer behavior.

### AI Exposure Is Difficult to Interpret

A high AI-exposure score does not necessarily mean that a job will disappear. AI may replace some tasks while increasing demand for other tasks. Decision-makers may interpret the same score differently.

### Data May Be Outdated

Official labor-market information is reliable, but some data may be released with a delay. AI technology and employer practices may change faster than traditional workforce statistics.

### Criteria May Conflict

An occupation may have high wages but require expensive training. Another occupation may have many openings but offer relatively low wages. A third occupation may have high AI exposure but uncertain future demand.

There may not be one occupation that performs best across every measure.

### Weighting the Criteria Is Subjective

The final result may depend heavily on how the decision-maker weights AI exposure, job demand, wages, equity, training cost, and workforce size.

Small changes in the weights could change the ranking.

### Stakeholder Opinions May Conflict

Employers, workers, training providers, and government agencies may have different priorities. Reaching agreement may require several meetings and revisions.

### Manual Analysis Can Be Slow

If the information is stored across different spreadsheets, staff may need to repeat the same cleaning, matching, and scoring work whenever new data becomes available.

### The Decision May Be Difficult to Explain

If the ranking is produced through several spreadsheets and informal judgments, stakeholders may not clearly understand why one occupation received funding while another did not.

---

## How Our Proposed Tool Would Improve the Process

The AI Workforce Investment Planner would not completely replace the decision-maker. Instead, it would organize the available evidence and make the decision process more consistent and transparent.

The tool could:

* Combine important workforce indicators in one place;
* Match information by occupation code;
* Compare occupations using consistent criteria;
* Allow users to adjust the importance of different criteria;
* Display the reasons behind each occupation’s score;
* Identify missing or outdated data;
* Compare statewide and regional needs;
* Support scenario analysis;
* Save time spent creating manual spreadsheets; and
* Produce results that are easier to explain to stakeholders.

For example, a user could increase the importance of equity or displacement risk and see how the priority list changes. This would allow the decision-maker to compare different policy goals instead of relying on only one fixed ranking.

The final funding decision would still require human judgment, stakeholder consultation, and policy review. The tool would support the decision rather than make the final decision automatically.

## Conclusion

Without the AI Workforce Investment Planner, the EDD decision-maker could still prioritize occupations by collecting labor-market data, consulting stakeholders, comparing occupations in spreadsheets, and using professional judgment.

However, the process would likely require information from many different sources. It could be slow, difficult to update, and influenced by subjective choices about which criteria are most important.

Our proposed tool would improve the current alternative by organizing the evidence, making comparisons easier, and showing how different policy priorities affect the results. It would not replace the decision-maker. Instead, it would help the decision-maker make a faster, more transparent, and more evidence-based funding recommendation.
