# Objectives Analysis

## Selected project area

AI Workforce Investment Planner

## Candidate Vision A

**What:** Show an evidence-based spectrum of occupations with different levels and types of AI exposure, then recommend priorities for AI-resilience career guidance and skill development. The product should not claim that exposure proves that a job will be replaced.

**For whom:** Recruiters and job applicants using LinkedIn Talent Solutions.

**Working decision-maker:** A product strategy leader at LinkedIn Talent Solutions must decide which occupations should be prioritized for AI-resilience career guidance and skill-development recommendations.

**Why it matters:** Recruiters and applicants need understandable information about how AI may change tasks and skills so they can make better hiring, career, and development decisions.

### Verified organizational and data context

-   LinkedIn says Talent Insights is designed to track talent and skills trends, address skill gaps, and support workforce planning using supply-and-demand insights. [LinkedIn Talent Insights](https://business.linkedin.com/hire/talent-insights)

-   LinkedIn explains that Talent Insights uses aggregated and standardized information from member profiles, including job titles, employers, skills, and education. [LinkedIn Talent Insights](https://business.linkedin.com/hire/talent-insights)

-   The Anthropic Economic Index maps observed Claude usage to occupational tasks and distinguishes between automation and augmentation. Anthropic also warns that its usage data is not representative of all AI use and does not forecast which occupations will disappear. [Anthropic Economic Index](https://www.anthropic.com/research/the-anthropic-economic-index)

-   ONET provides occupation descriptions, task statements, skills, work activities, education, experience, and training information organized using ONET-SOC occupation codes. [O\*NET Database](https://www.onetcenter.org/database.html)

### AI-generated hypotheses to investigate

The following objectives are hypotheses about what a LinkedIn product strategy leader, recruiter, or applicant may care about. They have not been confirmed through stakeholder interviews.

#### Desired outcomes

-   Help applicants identify occupations and skills that may remain valuable as AI changes work activities.

-   Help recruiters understand which skills may become more important within changing occupations.

-   Present AI exposure as a spectrum rather than a binary label such as “safe” or “replaced.”

-   Distinguish tasks more likely to be augmented by AI from tasks that appear more automatable.

-   Provide clear skill-development recommendations connected to occupational tasks.

-   Make the evidence and uncertainty behind every recommendation understandable.

#### Risks if the decision is wrong

-   Applicants could avoid viable careers because an occupation is inaccurately labeled as likely to disappear.

-   Recruiters could undervalue qualified candidates or overemphasize AI-related skills.

-   The product could confuse observed AI usage with technical automation potential or future job loss.

-   National occupation averages could hide differences among employers, industries, locations, and individual jobs.

-   Historical or platform-specific usage patterns could reinforce existing inequalities or under-represent occupations with less access to generative AI.

-   Recommendations could become outdated as AI capabilities, adoption, and labor-market conditions change.

#### Tradeoffs and constraints

-   Simple, accessible guidance versus a more accurate but complex explanation of uncertainty.

-   Personalized recommendations versus user privacy and limited access to reliable individual-level information.

-   Fast product updates versus sufficient validation of new data and methodology.

-   Broad national coverage versus differences across states, industries, and employers.

-   Career opportunity messaging versus the risk of creating fear about job displacement.

-   Recruiter usefulness versus fairness to applicants and workers.

#### Evidence needed

-   Observed AI usage or task-penetration measures by work activity or occupation.

-   The share of usage categorized as augmentation versus automation.

-   O\*NET task, skill, work-activity, and training requirements by occupation.

-   BLS employment, wage, job-opening, and projection measures.

-   Documentation showing how occupation and task identifiers are matched across sources.

-   User research showing that recruiters and applicants understand and use the recommendations appropriately.

-   Validation showing that the results are not merely reflecting the usage patterns of one AI platform.

#### Evidence that would change the decision-maker’s mind

-   A low or unreliable match rate between AI-use observations and occupation codes would weaken the case for occupation rankings.

-   Large disagreements between Anthropic and OpenAI usage patterns would suggest that one combined score may hide important differences.

-   User testing showing that people interpret “AI exposure” as “job elimination” would require different terminology or product design.

-   Evidence that occupation-level recommendations do not improve career or hiring decisions would reduce the value of the proposed feature.

-   Evidence of substantial demographic, geographic, or occupational bias would require safeguards, narrower claims, or reconsideration of the product.

#### Measures of success

-   Users can correctly distinguish AI exposure, augmentation, automation, and job displacement.

-   Applicants use the tool to identify relevant skills or training options rather than simply avoiding highly exposed occupations.

-   Recruiters report that the information improves skills-based workforce planning.

-   Recommendations include transparent source, date, methodology, and uncertainty information.

-   Error, coverage, and disagreement rates across datasets remain within thresholds established before launch.

-   Audits find no unacceptable disparities in how occupations or worker groups are represented.

### Questions for real stakeholders

1.  Is a product strategy leader at LinkedIn Talent Solutions the correct decision-maker, or should the target be a recruiter, career-services leader, or job seeker?

2.  What action should a user take after viewing an occupation’s AI exposure?

3.  What level of evidence would LinkedIn require before displaying an AI-resilience recommendation?

4.  How should the product communicate uncertainty without making the guidance unusably complex?

5.  What outcomes and fairness measures would LinkedIn use to judge success?

## Candidate Vision B

**What:** Prioritize occupations for public investment in AI-related reskilling programs.

**For whom:** The California Employment Development Department and its workforce and training partners.

**Working decision-maker:** A workforce development director at the California Employment Development Department must decide which occupations should receive priority funding for AI-related reskilling programs.

**Why it matters:** Public training resources are limited, so funding decisions should consider both how AI is changing work and how many workers could benefit from accessible, labor-market-relevant training.

### Verified organizational and data context

-   California EDD provides employment and training services for job seekers, employers, and workforce partners. It also provides labor-market information and resources intended to address California workforce skill-development needs. [California EDD Jobs and Training](https://edd.ca.gov/en/jobs_and_training/)

-   California EDD publishes workforce development funding opportunities through solicitations for proposals. [California EDD Workforce Development Solicitations](https://edd.ca.gov/en/Jobs_and_Training/WDSFP_Workforce_Development_Solicitations_for_Proposals)

-   BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics provides occupation-level employment and wage estimates, including state tables. [BLS OEWS Tables](https://www.bls.gov/oes/tables.htm)

-   BLS employment projections provide occupation-level measures such as employment change, openings, education, training, and wages. The team must verify whether a selected projection is sufficiently specific to California. [BLS Occupational Projections Data](https://www.bls.gov/emp/data/occupational-data.htm)

-   O\*NET provides occupation-level task, skill, work-activity, education, experience, and training information that could help interpret what reskilling an occupation may require. [O\*NET Database](https://www.onetcenter.org/database.html)

### AI-generated hypotheses to investigate

The following objectives are hypotheses about the California EDD decision process. They have not been confirmed by an EDD employee or an official program document describing this exact proposed decision.

#### Desired outcomes

-   Direct limited funding toward occupations in which workers face meaningful changes in tasks and skill requirements.

-   Support training that helps workers use AI as an augmenting tool where appropriate.

-   Improve participants’ employment, advancement, wage, or job-stability outcomes.

-   Align training with actual California employer demand and occupational opportunities.

-   Make programs accessible to workers and communities with the greatest need.

-   Produce transparent funding priorities that workforce partners can understand and challenge.

#### Risks if the decision is wrong

-   Funding could go to highly exposed occupations that employ few Californians or offer weak future opportunities.

-   Workers could be trained in skills that employers do not need.

-   A statewide ranking could overlook important regional differences within California.

-   Communities with limited access to training, technology, transportation, or broadband could receive less benefit.

-   The agency could treat AI exposure as proof of displacement even when the data only records AI use.

-   An occupation score could hide different needs among workers performing different tasks within the same occupation.

#### Tradeoffs and constraints

-   Supporting occupations at immediate risk versus investing in occupations with strong long-term growth.

-   Funding a small number of intensive programs versus providing broader access to lower-cost training.

-   Statewide consistency versus regional and industry-specific labor-market needs.

-   Training for AI tool use versus training for transitions into different occupations.

-   Speed of funding versus time needed to validate evidence and training quality.

-   Economic efficiency versus equitable access for underserved populations.

-   National AI-use evidence versus California-specific employment conditions.

#### Evidence needed

-   Occupation-level AI task penetration and automation-versus-augmentation measures.

-   California employment levels and wages by occupation.

-   Occupational projections, annual openings, and employer demand relevant to California.

-   O\*NET tasks, skills, work activities, job zones, education, and training requirements.

-   Reliable crosswalks among O\*NET-SOC and BLS SOC-based occupation classifications.

-   Information about training cost, duration, provider capacity, eligibility, accessibility, and completion.

-   Participant outcomes such as completion, employment, retention, advancement, and wages.

-   Distributional evidence showing which regions and worker groups would receive the benefits.

#### Evidence that would change the decision-maker’s mind

-   If AI-use data cannot be matched reliably to California occupations, it should not drive funding rankings.

-   If an occupation has high AI exposure but low California employment or weak demand, it may deserve lower priority.

-   If an occupation has high augmentation potential and strong demand, training may be more appropriate than transition assistance.

-   If training providers cannot deliver accessible, relevant programs, funding the occupation may not produce meaningful results.

-   If pilot participants do not improve their employment, wage, retention, or skill outcomes, the program design or occupation priority should change.

-   If benefits are concentrated in already advantaged groups, the agency may need different outreach, eligibility, or funding criteria.

#### Measures of success

-   Training enrollment and completion rates.

-   Employment, retention, advancement, or wage improvement after training.

-   Evidence that participants use the targeted AI-related skills in their work.

-   Employer satisfaction with the relevance of participants’ skills.

-   Equitable access and outcomes across regions and worker populations.

-   Cost per successful participant outcome.

-   Transparent and reproducible occupation-priority scores.

-   Regular reassessment as AI-use and labor-market evidence changes.

### Questions for real stakeholders

1.  Does California EDD directly make this funding decision, or is authority shared with another state board, local workforce boards, or training partners?

2.  What funding program, budget, eligibility rules, and decision schedule would constrain the analysis?

3.  Does EDD prioritize occupations, worker populations, regions, industries, or some combination of these?

4.  What participant outcome would count as success, and over what time period?

5.  How should equity be incorporated into the priority criteria?

6.  What California-specific data and program-performance information could be made available?

## Comparison of the Candidate Visions

### Relevance

-   **Vision A** could help recruiters and applicants interpret changing occupational skills, but the proposed audience contains multiple users with different decisions. The team must identify one primary decision-maker and one concrete action.

-   **Vision B** has a clearer resource-allocation decision: determine which occupations should receive priority funding for reskilling. Its public purpose and measures of success are easier to state.

### Feasibility

-   **Vision A** would benefit from LinkedIn-specific member, job-posting, skill, and hiring data that may not be publicly available. With only public data, the team could build a general career-guidance prototype but should not claim that it represents LinkedIn’s users or labor market.

-   **Vision B** is more compatible with public occupation-level sources. Anthropic or OpenAI can contribute AI-use measures, O\*NET can contribute occupational tasks and skill requirements, and BLS can contribute state employment and wage information. Important gaps remain around California-specific projections, training availability, program costs, and participant outcomes.

### Preliminary conclusion

Vision B currently appears more feasible and decision-focused because its stakeholder, decision, and public datasets align more clearly. Vision A remains promising if the team narrows the audience and reframes the output as AI-resilience career and skill guidance rather than a prediction of which jobs will be “taken over.”
